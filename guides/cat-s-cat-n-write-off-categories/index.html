Cat S and Cat N explained: what write-off categories mean when you're buying a car

By Aniket Chakraborty, founder of Livery · Updated 9 November 2026

A Cat S or Cat N car can be a bargain or a money pit. The category tells you what kind of damage the car had, but not how well it was put right. Here's what each category means and how to check the rest.

The four categories at a glance

When an insurer decides a damaged car isn't worth repairing, it's "written off" and given a category under the motor insurance industry's salvage code of practice. The category tells everyone who buys it afterwards how bad the damage was.

CategoryWhat it meansBack on the road?
Category A – Scrap onlySo badly damaged it must be crushed, whole. No parts can be reused.Never
Category B – Break for partsThe body shell must be crushed, but some parts can be taken off and sold.Never
Category S – Structural damageThe structural frame or chassis was damaged, for example a bent chassis leg or crumpled crash structure. It can be professionally repaired.Yes, once repaired
Category N – Non-structural damageNo structural damage, but the insurer chose not to repair it. That can be cosmetic, electrical or safety-related, such as airbags or brakes.Yes, once repaired

If a Cat A or Cat B car is for sale as a road car, walk away. These cars should never be driven again, and DVLA won't re-register them. A car being sold whole with one of these markers may have a cloned identity.

Why cars get written off, and why it isn't always serious

Insurers write off a car when repairing it doesn't make financial sense. That's partly about how bad the damage is and partly about money: the cost of repair compared with the car's value, plus the cost of a hire car while it's in the bodyshop and what the insurer gets back for selling the salvage.

So an older car with modest damage can be written off simply because it isn't worth much, while a newer car with worse damage might be repaired. A Cat N car with a scuffed bumper and a dented door is a very different thing from a Cat S car that's been in a heavy front-end crash. The category is where your questions start, not where they end.

Cat C and Cat D: the old categories

Before October 2017, repairable write-offs were Category C and Category D. They were based on cost, not on what was damaged:

  • Cat C: repairable, but repairs would have cost more than the car was worth.
  • Cat D: repairable, and repairs would have cost less than the car was worth, but the insurer decided a payout made more sense.

S and N replaced C and D for cars written off from 1 October 2017. They aren't a straight swap: an old Cat D car could have had structural damage, and an old Cat C car might not have. Cars written off before then keep their old category, so you'll still see C and D on older cars.

How to find out if a car has been written off

Write-offs are recorded on a national register that insurers share, and a full history check reads it. The V5C logbook may not tell you, and a free MOT or tax check won't.

A dealer selling a written-off car must tell you, and can't hide known accident damage. A private seller doesn't have to volunteer it, but mustn't lie about it if you ask. So ask directly, in writing if you can, and check for yourself either way.

Should you buy a Cat S or Cat N car?

GOV.UK's own advice is that there's nothing wrong with buying a written-off car that has been properly returned to good condition. Plenty of people do, and the lower price can be good value. The risk is in how well it was repaired, and that's what to check.

Questions to ask the seller

  • What exactly was damaged, and how did it happen?
  • Who repaired it? A specialist bodyshop working to the manufacturer's repair methods, or a home job?
  • Can I see the invoices, the parts list and any photos of the damage and the repair?
  • For Cat S: was the structural repair checked afterwards, for example with a wheel alignment and a jig or measurement report?
  • For Cat N: if airbags went off, were they replaced with new ones, and have the airbag and seatbelt systems been checked?

Get it inspected

For a Cat S car especially, pay for an independent inspection from an engineer before you buy. They'll check the structure, the alignment, the welding and the panel fit, and often find faults the seller didn't know about. It costs far less than buying a badly repaired car.

Get an insurance quote first

Some insurers won't cover a previously written-off car, and others charge more or ask for an engineer's report. Get a real quote, telling the insurer the category, before you agree a price. You must disclose it: not mentioning a write-off can invalidate your cover.

What a write-off does to value

A Cat S or Cat N car is worth noticeably less than the same car with a clean history, and that gap doesn't go away. It's a good deal when you buy, but you'll get less when you sell, and you'll have to tell the next buyer too. Structural (Cat S) damage generally knocks more off than non-structural (Cat N). Price it against other write-offs, not against clean cars.

If you already own one

Keep the repair invoices and inspection report with the car's history, because the next buyer will ask for them. Tell your insurer about the category at every renewal. If your own car is written off, your insurer handles the paperwork with DVLA when it takes the car, and you can sometimes buy a repairable (S or N) car back from the insurer and repair it yourself.

Questions people ask

What does Cat N mean on a car?

Category N means an insurer wrote the car off but it had no structural damage. The damage was to things like panels, lights, trim, electrics or airbags. It can be repaired and driven again, but it's usually worth less and some insurers charge more to cover it.

What does Cat S mean on a car?

Category S means the car's structural frame or chassis was damaged and an insurer wrote it off, but it can be professionally repaired and driven again. It's worth getting an independent inspection before buying one.

Is it safe to buy a Cat S or Cat N car?

It can be, if it was repaired properly. Ask for repair invoices and photos, get an independent inspection, especially for Cat S, and get an insurance quote before you buy.

Do I have to tell my insurer my car is Cat S or Cat N?

Yes. You need to declare it when you take out or renew a policy. Not mentioning it can invalidate your insurance.

What replaced Cat C and Cat D?

Categories S and N replaced C and D for cars written off from 1 October 2017. C and D were based on repair cost; S and N are based on whether the structure was damaged. Cars written off before then keep their old category.
